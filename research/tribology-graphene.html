Wear Resistance of Graphene Coatings on Aluminium.

Tribometer testing of carboxyl-functionalized graphene nanoplatelet (GNP-COOH) additive in polyurethane coatings on aluminium, benchmarked against PTFE-based CeraFlour, with applied writeups on where graphene coatings are useful in tires and rotary blades.

Freire-Gormaly Lab, York University · Researcher · April 2024 to August 2025

01

What It Was

The starting point was Ceraflour 999, a PTFE (Teflon) based additive already used to give polyurethane coatings good wear resistance and low friction. PTFE has drawbacks, so the question was whether a carboxyl-functionalized graphene nanoplatelet additive (GNP-COOH) could get close to Ceraflour's performance in a polyurethane coating on aluminium, without relying on PTFE.

Each formulation was applied to aluminium coupons and run through a 10 N linear wear test on a tribometer. Two things came out of each run: a mean coefficient of friction (COF), and the mass lost from the coating over the test. Comparing formulations on both measures gives a read on which coatings actually protect the surface rather than just looking different under the microscope.

02

Coating Formulations

Six systems, three CeraFlour types with and without GNP-COOH

All coatings were built on an Acry Glo polyurethane base and hardener, mixed 3:1. Additive loadings were run at 0.5%, 1% and 3% w/v. GNP-COOH batches added dispersing and wetting agents (BYK-A 501, BYK-C 8001, DisperBYK) and were sonicated for 30 minutes before mixing in the hardener, then applied to the aluminium substrate.

  • 1052 CeraFlour PU — a Teflon-free CeraFlour type
  • 999 CeraFlour PU — the PTFE/Teflon-based benchmark
  • 1051 CeraFlour PU — a second Teflon-free CeraFlour type
  • GNP-COOH with 1052, 999 and 1051 CeraFlour PU — each CeraFlour type with graphene additive added

Sample codes encode concentration, base chemistry and replicate number, for example 3013-2 is a 3% w/v, polyurethane-base, 1052-type sample, replicate 2.

The 1051 CeraFlour PU system, without graphene, gave the most consistent combination of low COF and low mass loss. Adding GNP-COOH generally raised COF and gave mixed results on mass loss, better on the 999 system, worse on 1052. On this data, GNP-COOH did not clearly outperform the Teflon-free CeraFlour types it was meant to help, though it did outperform the plain PU and graphene-only baselines. What I Did

  • Formulated and mixed six coating systems, plain PU, three CeraFlour types, and three GNP-COOH plus CeraFlour combinations
  • Applied coatings to aluminium coupons and ran tribometer wear tests across 27 samples
  • Measured coefficient of friction and mass loss for each coating
  • Processed the raw tribometer output through a tribology analysis script
  • Wrote up applied cases for graphene coatings on rubber tires and rotary blades, arguing why the same wear and friction behaviour is useful in those applications

05

Where This Applies: Tires and Rotary Blades

The same wear and friction behaviour, two very different products

Rotary blades

Wind turbine and aircraft rotor blades take continuous leading-edge erosion from rain, dust and sand, and minor repairs already cost a large multiple of what a major repair or replacement runs. Graphene's high surface hardness and large surface area improve coating adhesion and abrasion resistance, and its self-lubricating behaviour lowers the friction that drives that erosion, extending blade life and cutting maintenance frequency.

Rubber tires

Roughly 5.1 billion tires are discarded globally every year, and frequent replacement is part of why. Graphene's high surface area and aspect ratio reinforce the rubber matrix, improving abrasion resistance and extending tire life, while lower rolling resistance from the same additive improves fuel efficiency, both of which cut how often a tire needs replacing in the first place.
