Engineered Parking, Smart Parking Guidance with Gas Sensing.

A LabVIEW and Arduino parking lot system that opens a column's gate and lights its LEDs the moment a spot frees up in it, with a fully independent MQ-2 ventilation circuit that fans out hazardous fume levels regardless of what the parking logic is doing.

Course project, Engineered Parking · LabVIEW and Arduino

01

What It Was

Up to 30% of urban traffic is drivers searching for parking, averaging 8.5 minutes per search (Shoup, 2006), and idling vehicles account for roughly 1.6% of urban CO&sub2; emissions (Paidi et al., 2022). The brief was a smart parking system that cuts that search time and monitors air quality at the same time: light-dependent resistors (LDRs) detect occupancy, LabVIEW processes it, servo gates and LEDs guide drivers in, and a separate MQ-2 gas-sensing circuit runs a ventilation fan.

The original brief called for guiding drivers to a specific free spot and prioritising whichever column had more availability, both of which were cut for time and budget. Spot-level guidance would have needed several SPDT relays or solid-state switches and a redesigned LED circuit; column prioritisation would have needed LabVIEW counting cars per column from Boolean values and driving a decision structure off that count. The final design instead opens a column's gate and lights its LEDs as soon as any spot frees up in it, a scoped-down design that still gets a driver to a free spot without the added complexity, and left enough time to build and clean the hardware before the demo.

03

System Logic

A voltage comparator per spot, two independent circuits overall

Each LDR sits in a voltage-divider comparator circuit against a 10 kΩ reference resistor. LabVIEW reads the divider output through a "greater than" block and converts it to a Boolean: true means the spot is taken, false means it is free. Column A reads analog inputs 0–3, Column B reads 4–7. A compound arithmetic block ANDs each column's four Booleans together, and when a column reads fully occupied its case structure closes that column's gate and holds its LEDs off; a second compound arithmetic block ANDs both columns together so the main gate stays closed only once the whole lot is full.

The ventilation circuit is deliberately not part of this logic. It runs on its own Arduino Nano, separate from the Arduino Uno and power supply driving the parking logic, so a fault in the gate and LED logic can't take the fan down with it. The MQ-2 output is read continuously and compared against a fixed threshold in the Arduino sketch; above it, the fan relay switches on.

Challenges and Resolutions

What went wrong on the bench, and what fixed it

  • Printing failures. Early servo mounts didn't account for motor depth, and the legs' pegs came up short once drilling debris was cleared. Reprinted with a snug motor pocket fastened by an M2 bolt and nut.
  • Ultrasonic sensor failure at the main gate. It would intermittently read a bad value and fault the system. Replaced with another LDR on the same voltage-comparator circuit already used elsewhere, wired to the Arduino Uno's A0 pin since the Q-8 DAQ's eight analog inputs were already committed to the parking spaces.
  • Acrylic drilling. Acrylic's low melting point means it melts as it's drilled, worst with the larger 0.25″ bit used for the support pegs. Bored carefully rather than switching material.

Ambient light variation also affected the LDR readings during the demo; it was resolved live by recalibrating the reference voltage to the room's lighting rather than a fix in the logic itself. An overhead LED array to control the lighting environment was the follow-up fix identified but not built.
